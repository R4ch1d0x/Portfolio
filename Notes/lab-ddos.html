Chapter 1 of 75 min

What are DoS and DDoS?

After this chapter you can:
  • define DoS and DDoS in one sentence each
  • explain why distribution makes defence harder
  • name the three resources an attacker tries to exhaust

Denial of Service DoS

An attack on availability. One source, usually a single machine or connection, makes a service slow or unreachable for its legitimate users. It either overloads a resource the service depends on or triggers a flaw that makes it crash.

Distributed Denial of Service DDoS

The same goal, but the traffic comes from many machines at once. They are usually a botnet: thousands of compromised PCs, servers, routers or IoT devices obeying a command-and-control (C2) server. Rented attack services and abused third-party servers can play the same role.

What gets exhausted?

Bandwidth

The link to the internet has a fixed size. When junk fills it, legitimate packets are dropped before they reach the server. This is the volumetric goal.

State

Servers, firewalls and load balancers remember each connection. Their tables have a limit. When it is full, no new client can connect, even if the link is empty.

Compute

Web workers, CPU and database queries do real work for each request. A small number of expensive requests can keep every worker busy.

Key takeaways

  • DoS comes from one source. DDoS comes from many sources acting together.
  • Distribution defeats the simplest defence, which is blocking the attacker's address.
  • Attackers exhaust bandwidth, connection state or application compute.
Chapter 2 of 77 min

Classifying DDoS attacks on two axes

After this chapter you can:
  • place any attack by technical family and by mechanism
  • explain the difference between reflection and amplification
  • say which defences work at the source of the problem

Volume is the weapon, but an attack can be described in two complementary ways. The first axis asks which layer of the network stack is targeted. The second asks how the traffic reaches the victim.

Axis 1 · technical family

Protocol attacks L3L4

They target the network and transport layers: IP, ICMP, TCP and UDP. They exhaust bandwidth (volumetric floods) or connection state (SYN flood). Packets are cheap to generate, so volumes can be very large.

Examples: ICMP flood, UDP flood, SYN flood, DNS or NTP reflection.

Application attacks L7

They target the application: HTTP, DNS queries, APIs. Requests look legitimate and complete the TCP handshake, so volumes are small, but each request is expensive for the server.

Examples: HTTP GET or POST flood, Slowloris, login or search abuse.

Some sources list "volumetric" as a third family. Here volumetric describes a goal, saturating bandwidth, that is reached mostly at layers 3 and 4, so it sits inside the protocol family.

Axis 2 · mechanism

Direct

The attacking machines send traffic straight to the victim. The attacker controls every packet and the source addresses are the bots' own, or spoofed.

Victim sees: the bots' addresses.

Reflection

The attacker sends requests to third-party servers with the victim's address forged as the source. The servers answer the victim. This needs a protocol without a handshake, usually UDP.

Victim sees: innocent servers, not the attacker.

Amplification

A small request triggers a much larger response. The attacker spends little bandwidth and the victim receives many times more. It is almost always combined with reflection.

Victim sees: large responses it never asked for.

Reflection and amplification are different ideas. Reflection hides the attacker and recruits third parties. Amplification multiplies the volume. Together they let an attacker with a modest connection flood a large target.

Reflection only works if forged source addresses can leave the attacker's network. Ingress and egress filtering (BCP 38) blocks them at the source. Closing open resolvers and unused UDP services shrinks the supply of reflectors.

Key takeaways

  • Family answers "which layer?". Mechanism answers "how does the traffic arrive?".
  • Reflection uses forged source addresses. Amplification makes the reply bigger than the request.
  • L7 attacks need few packets but expensive requests, so volume alone does not reveal them.
Chapter 3 of 78 min

Attack vectors at layers 3, 4 and 7

After this chapter you can:
  • describe how five common vectors work
  • say which resource each one exhausts
  • recognise each vector by its traffic signature

Each vector abuses one weakness. For every card, note what runs out and what the packets look like. You will see all of them again in the live lab.

ICMP flood L3

Sends a huge number of ICMP Echo Requests (ping). The target has to receive and often answer each one, so bandwidth and CPU are consumed.

Signature: a flood of ICMP type 8 packets of identical size from many sources. Relatives: Smurf (ICMP reflection through broadcast addresses) and Ping of Death (oversized fragments, an old bug).

UDP flood L4

Sends UDP datagrams to random ports. The server finds no listener and replies with ICMP "port unreachable", so it pays bandwidth and CPU twice. UDP has no handshake, so sources are easy to forge.

Signature: high UDP packet rate to many different destination ports, with ICMP port unreachable going out.

SYN flood L4

Starts TCP handshakes and never finishes them. Each SYN makes the server reserve a half-open entry and send a SYN-ACK. When the backlog is full, real clients cannot connect. It exhausts connection state.

Signature: many SYN, almost no ACK. Many sockets in SYN_RECV. Repeated SYN-ACK retransmissions to random sources.

HTTP flood L7

Sends valid GET or POST requests, often to expensive endpoints such as search, login or checkout, and with random query strings to bypass the cache. It exhausts application compute.

Signature: same headers on every request, one URL pattern, many requests per IP, no cookies. Bandwidth is modest while CPU and database load spike.

Slowloris (slow HTTP) L7

Opens many connections and sends headers or bodies extremely slowly, never finishing. Each connection holds a web worker. A few hundred connections can lock a server using almost no bandwidth.

Signature: many long-lived connections with tiny segments. Workers saturated while network traffic looks normal.

Also seen in the wild

Key takeaways

  • ICMP and UDP floods fill the pipe. SYN floods fill the connection table. HTTP floods and Slowloris fill the workers.
  • A low-bandwidth attack can still take a service down. Do not rely on traffic volume alone.
  • Each vector has a signature you can see in a packet capture. Chapter 4 shows them.

    Chapter 5 of 76 min

    Detecting a DDoS attack

    After this chapter you can:
    • explain why a baseline comes first
    • list detection signals for each layer
    • tell an attack from a legitimate traffic surge

    Detection means noticing that traffic or behaviour left its normal range, then confirming that it is hostile. You can only see a deviation if you know what normal looks like.

    Signals by layer

    LayerWhat to watch
    L3Bandwidth and packets per second above baseline. Protocol mix shifts toward ICMP or UDP. Many new source addresses.
    L4SYN count far above ACK count. Many sockets in SYN_RECV. Connection table or NAT table near its limit. Inbound UDP from source ports 53, 123, 1900, 11211.
    L7Requests per IP, identical User-Agent or URL patterns, no cookies, rising 5xx errors and latency, workers saturated while bandwidth is low.

    Build a baseline

    Record normal packets, connections and requests per second by hour and day, plus usual countries and client types. Alert on deviation from that profile, not on one fixed number.

    Use two signals

    Volume alone also fires on a product launch. Combine volume with a second signal such as client diversity, SYN to ACK ratio or error rate before declaring an attack.

    Watch the user view

    Synthetic checks that load your site from outside tell you what users feel. Slow-and-low attacks can hurt users while network graphs look calm.

    Where the data comes from

    • Flow data: NetFlow, IPFIX or sFlow from routers show who talks to whom and how much.
    • Packet capture: tcpdump or Wireshark for a close look at a short window.
    • IDS and IPS: Suricata rules and thresholds for floods and malformed traffic.
    • Application logs: web server, WAF and load balancer logs feed a SIEM such as Wazuh with ELK for dashboards and alerts.
    • Host metrics: CPU, memory, socket states and worker usage.

    A simple alert rule

    # pseudo-rule, evaluated every 10 s
if syn_rate   > 5 * baseline_syn
and ack_ratio < 0.20
and unique_sources > 2000
then alert("Possible SYN flood")

# application layer
if rps_per_ip > 50
and cookie_present == false
and p95_latency > 3 * baseline
then alert("Possible HTTP flood")

    Key takeaways

    • Detection starts with a baseline and alerts on deviation from it.
    • Layer 4 and layer 7 attacks have their own signals. Network volume misses low-and-slow attacks.
    • Use at least two signals to separate attacks from flash crowds.
    Chapter 6 of 77 min

    Protecting against DDoS

    After this chapter you can:
    • describe a layered defence from the internet to the origin
    • match a defence to each attack vector
    • outline what to do before, during and after an attack

    No single control stops every attack. Defence works in layers: each layer removes the traffic it is best placed to recognise, so less reaches the next one.

    LegitimateVolumetric floodState exhaustion (SYN)Application flood
    WebGL is not available in this browser, so the 3D scene cannot run. The list next to it describes the same layers.
    1. Upstream and ISP. Anti-spoofing filters (BCP 38), blackholing and flow rules stop the worst traffic before it reaches you.
    2. Scrubbing and anycast CDN. Traffic is spread over many sites with huge capacity. Floods are absorbed and only clean traffic is forwarded.
    3. Firewall and edge. ACLs drop unused protocols and ports. SYN cookies and SYN rate limits protect connection state.
    4. WAF and rate limiting. Per-client limits, bot detection and JavaScript or CAPTCHA challenges separate bots from people.
    5. Origin. Caching, timeouts, connection limits and autoscaling with a budget cap keep the application alive.

    Match the defence to the vector

    VectorMain defenceAlso
    ICMP flood L3Rate-limit or drop ICMP echo at the edgeUpstream filtering, scrubbing for large floods
    UDP flood L4Drop UDP to ports with no service, rate-limitStateful firewall, scrubbing
    SYN flood L4SYN cookies, SYN rate limits, larger backlogProxy that completes the handshake first
    Reflection / amplification L4Drop unsolicited UDP from port 53, 123, 1900, 11211 and upstream scrubbingAt the source: BCP 38, close open resolvers, disable monlist, no public memcached
    HTTP flood L7WAF rules, per-IP rate limits, bot challengeCaching, protect expensive endpoints, require login for heavy actions
    Slowloris L7Short header and body timeouts, per-IP connection limitsReverse proxy or CDN that buffers requests

    Before

    Know your normal traffic. Put a CDN or protection service in front. Write a runbook with contacts at your ISP and provider. Close open services. Set rate limits, timeouts and a spending cap on autoscaling. Rehearse.

    During

    Confirm the attack with two signals. Identify the vector from captures and logs. Call the upstream provider and enable the matching mitigation. Keep a status page up to date. Keep notes and logs for later.

    After

    Review the timeline. Tune thresholds and rules. Fix the weakness that was hit. Update the runbook. Report the incident to the provider and, where required, to authorities.

    Ethics and law: test only systems you own or are explicitly authorised to test, in writing. Attacking other people's systems, including with rented "stress testing" services, is illegal in most countries.

    Key takeaways

    • Layer your defences. Capacity and filtering go upstream, application logic stays at the origin.
    • Every vector has a specific fix: SYN cookies, UDP filtering, WAF rate limits, timeouts.
    • Preparation decides the outcome. A runbook and provider contacts matter before the first packet arrives.
